STUDY STATUS
The thinking behind Framing Lab

Why I built
this experiment.

Marketing and Consumer Psychology are fascinating! A price is more than a number.

The price can signal quality, establish a reference point, and influence how consumers evaluate a product.

Decades of research in consumer psychology and marketing have examined these relationships. Tversky and Kahneman (1974) demonstrated how numerical anchors influence judgments, while Rao and Monroe (1989) documented the relationship between price and perceived quality.

Building on this research, Framing Lab explores a specific question: When people see different example prices for the same product, does their stated willingness to pay change? The experiment doesn't assume an effect. It tests whether one appears in our sample.

The question

For an identical hypothetical insulated bottle, does showing an example listed price of CHF 95 rather than CHF 45 change the maximum amount people say they would pay?

We hypothesize that the higher reference-price condition will have a higher average stated willingness to pay. The hypothesis is not the result; an unchanged or opposite difference is an informative possibility.

The setup

Population
Self-selected visitors to this public web experiment, not a representative Swiss sample
Random assignment
50/50 server-side assignment to CHF 45 or CHF 95, persisted for each anonymous participant ID
Held constant
Same illustration, specifications, question wording, answer control, and visual design
Primary outcome
Stated maximum willingness to pay in CHF; whole francs, 0–250 inclusive
Primary contrast
High-group mean WTP minus low-group mean WTP
Data hygiene
Only server-confirmed saves; one immutable recorded answer per participant ID, failed and invalid submissions excluded
Study status
Version 2 is separate from version 1. The start date, stopping rule, and when they were documented should be recorded transparently in the study protocol.

What counts as evidence?

We will compare group counts and WTP distributions, means and medians, then quantify uncertainty around the mean difference. Random assignment supports a comparison within people who participated, but the result doesn't automatically generalize to all shoppers.

Results are not exposed during active collection, to reduce the chance of influencing new respondents. Even when public results are released, they will remain explicitly descriptive and exploratory.

What this experiment doesn't tell us

  • A hypothetical answer is not equivalent to spending real money.
  • Example listed prices may convey quality information rather than generate pure anchoring bias.
  • People who find and complete the website may differ from the wider population.
  • Browser storage is not a strong identity mechanism; motivated participants could create multiple IDs.
  • We do not claim any commercial conversion or revenue uplift from this experiment.

This is a product project, not a recommendation for a real-world architecture

Building the interface is only one part. The product work includes narrowing the question, preserving an unbiased first interaction, making the server—not the browser—authoritative for assignment, verifying submissions, specifying measurements, and communicating uncertainty.

It is also an intentionally small system: static frontend on GitHub Pages, a Cloudflare Worker connecting a JSON API, and Google Apps Script storing response data in Google Sheets. That minimizes the setup.

From a marketing question to a product test

If we would be working with real-world business data and this test would be part of work with a brand: We would conduct an experiment with purchasable products which would have truthful and legally compliant price cues, measuring conversion, contribution margin and customer trust. The current experiment cannot replace that test. It showcases how a marketing question can be turned into a hypothesis with a testing framework.
